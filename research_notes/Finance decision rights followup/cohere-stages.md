## Q1. Who holds the decision right, under what legal vehicle and statutory authority, and is the next stage genuinely discretionary?

### Takeaway

The **legal vehicle and statutory capacity are now established from primary law**, and they are stronger than the earlier notes assumed: a Crown agent corporation with a natural-person contracting capacity can lawfully take equity and attach covenants. But the **named investor, the signatory, and the tranche structure are still not established by any government or company document**. Whether the next stage is *discretionary* is therefore not a question about the government's willingness — it is a question about whether the stages are one contract or several, and the public record cannot yet distinguish those two cases. That distinction decides the entire leverage claim.

### Cited Findings

**The announcement itself (primary, read in full).**

- Announced 29 October 2026, Montréal: "the Government of Canada's commitment to **making an equity investment of up to $1.6 billion in Cohere**. This is a **commercial investment**, announced alongside private investors, including **NVIDIA, Bell, and Schwarz Group**." — [PM of Canada release, 29 Oct 2026](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/10/29/canada-announces-landmark-investment-cohere-scale-secure-sovereign-ai)
- Company undertakings, in the release's own words: "Create at least **1,000** new high-paying Canadian technical jobs by **2030**"; "Commit at least **$1.7 billion** to additional computing infrastructure in Canada by 2030"; "Maintain its **Canadian incorporation and Canadian headquarters**"; research partnerships; "**Keep working with the Canadian AI Safety Institute** to champion the safe development and use of advanced AI." — same release
- Staging language: "It will be made **in stages, with regular reporting on progress against the company's commitments, including jobs and investment in Canada**. This approach will give Cohere the capital to expand and **hold the company accountable** to deliver benefits to Canadians." Quick facts: "will total up to approximately **$1.6 billion** and will be made in stages." — same release

**The legal vehicle (primary law, read in full).**

- The **Canada Development Investment Corporation** was continued as a statutory Crown corporation by the [Canada Development Investment Corporation Act, S.C. 2026, c. 3, s. 386](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-3.43/FullText.html), assented 26 March 2026, **in force 14 June 2026** (SI/2026-26). Act current to 30 September 2026.
- s.7: "The Corporation is for all purposes an **agent of His Majesty in right of Canada**." s.9: "the Corporation has the **capacity, rights, powers and privileges of a natural person**." — same Act
- s.10(1): "The mandate of the Corporation is to assist in the creation and development of businesses, resources, property and industries of Canada." s.10(2): "In carrying out its mandate, the Corporation **must conduct all of its activities in the best interests of Canada and must do so in a commercial manner**." — same Act
- s.11(b): the Corporation "may **invest in entities** owning property or carrying on business related to the economic interests of Canada, including by **acquiring their shares or securities**." — same Act
- s.21(1): "any information obtained by the Corporation or any of its subsidiaries in relation to entities in which they hold investments, **other than wholly-owned subsidiaries, is privileged**" and must not be disclosed; s.21(2) permits disclosure only for specified administrative/enforcement purposes or "**with the written consent of the person to which it relates**." — same Act
- s.22(1)–(2): "The **authorized capital** of the Corporation is an amount **determined by the Minister of Finance**"; if the Board recommends, the designated Minister "may … subscribe at par for the number of shares that the designated Minister considers desirable, and the amount of each subscription is to be paid … **out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund** at the times and in the amounts that the Board requires." s.23 allows the Minister of Finance to lend to the Corporation out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund. — same Act

### Inferences

- **Currency.** The government release writes "$1.6 billion" with no unit. Because Cohere's own release denominates the round in **CAD** and Reuters converts C$7bn to US$4.49bn, the Canadian commitment is **CAD by strong inference (≈US$1.03bn)**. It is still not stated by the government source, and the ceiling is hedged inconsistently inside one document ("up to $1.6 billion" and "up to approximately $1.6 billion").
- **Budget authority is the sharper question, not the amount.** The $1.6bn ceiling is **more than three times** the entire $500m Canadian Tech Growth Fund announced a week earlier. The two statements can coexist only if the Cohere stake is funded outside that fund — most plausibly through CDEV's own authorized capital under s.22, which is "an amount determined by the Minister of Finance" and drawn from the Consolidated Revenue Fund at the Board's request. If that is how it is structured, **no deal-specific parliamentary vote is required** and the amount may never appear as a line item. This is an inference from statutory text only; CDEV's authorized capital figure was not located.
- **The "in stages" question has two incompatible answers.** (a) One subscription agreement with staged capital calls: later stages are **already contractually committed**, withholding is a default, and the discretion has been spent at signature. (b) Separate per-stage subscription agreements: each stage is a **fresh decision by the holder**, and the discretion is real. The PM release's phrase "hold the company accountable" is consistent with both, and is political language, not a remedy. **No public evidence distinguishes them.**
- **The statutorily privileged information rule, not commercial confidentiality, is the reason this will stay secret.** s.21(1) of the CDEV Act makes information about non-wholly-owned investees privileged, releasable essentially only with the investee's written consent. So even a well-formed freedom-of-information request should be expected to fail, and the practical route is parliamentary/committee disclosure or a ministerial statement, not document release.
- **Canada Growth Fund and Canada Innovation Corporation are not supported here.** CGF is a CDEV subsidiary, but nothing located ties it to the Cohere round. The Canada Innovation Corporation did not appear in any document retrieved. Reporting, and the programmatic evidence, point to CDEV and to BDC-linked programmes instead. Both hypotheses should be dropped rather than carried as "possible vehicles".
- **Co-investor context reduces the government's relative weight but does not prove substitutability.** Schwarz Group leads; Bell Ventures, Bullhound, Inovia, NVIDIA and Prosperity7 also participate. A round that reached an initial close at a C$35.7bn valuation in a strong AI market implies Cohere has alternatives. This is a directional argument only; no substitute-cost evidence was found.

### Gaps

- **Named investor and signatory.** No government, CDEV, Cohere or Crown-agency document names the investing entity or the person who signs. The CDEV attribution is a single secondary report relayed through an aggregator, whose underlying article was unreachable.
- **Tranche schedule, stage amounts, and whether the paid portion is disclosed.** None of these appear anywhere retrieved. "Paid out in stages tied to … revenue targets" (secondary) adds a covenant the government release does not mention; the government text lists jobs and investment only.
- **Board seat, reserved matters, information rights.** The release mentions no governance right. Equity does not imply a board seat, and a board seat would not imply a model-release veto.
- **Whether the investment has actually been signed at all.** The release uses "commitment", "planned landmark investment", and "will be made". Cohere's release uses "commitments to date". Nothing establishes an executed subscription.

## Q2. Can that instrument legally bear an enforceable safety term — and what is the strongest plausible condition versus the evidenced current duty?

### Takeaway

**Yes on legal capacity, no on current content.** The statute permits the holder to contract, to take shares, and to accept covenants; it also imposes a **"commercial manner" duty** that constrains how a safety term must be framed. The only safety content in evidence is "keep working with CAISI", and CAISI is a **$50m research funder housed in ISED with no inspection, licensing, or model-access power** — so that sentence cannot carry an enforceable deliverable on its own. The gap is therefore not legal impossibility but an unwritten term.

### Cited Findings

**Capacity to bear a condition.**

- s.9 gives the Corporation "the capacity, rights, powers and privileges of a natural person"; s.8 allows it and its agent subsidiaries to "enter into contracts with His Majesty as though they were not an agent"; s.11(b) permits acquiring shares or securities. There is **no provision prohibiting non-financial covenants**. — [CDEV Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-3.43/FullText.html)
- The countervailing provision is s.10(2): the Corporation "**must** conduct all of its activities in the best interests of Canada and **must do so in a commercial manner**." A condition that raises the investee's cost or risk with no commercial rationale is exposed to this duty; a condition framed as protecting the value, security and service continuity of the asset is not. — same Act
- s.17 separately authorises guarantees "in accordance with any terms or conditions specified by the Minister of Finance" — evidence that Parliament contemplated the Minister attaching conditions to CDEV's financial instruments, though that section addresses guarantees, not equity. — same Act

**The named safety counterparty has no coercive power.**

- CAISI was launched 12 November 2024: "an **initial budget of $50 million over five years**"; it "will be **housed at Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada**"; research is delivered "through a **contribution agreement** with … CIFAR" and "**government-directed projects** … implemented by the National Research Council of Canada". — [ISED, Canada launches CAISI, 12 Nov 2024](https://www.canada.ca/en/innovation-science-economic-development/news/2024/11/canada-launches-canadian-artificial-intelligence-safety-institute.html)
- It is described as part of a strategy "which includes the **proposed** Artificial Intelligence and Data Act and the **Voluntary** Code of Conduct on the Responsible Development and Management of Advanced Generative AI Systems." Both instruments are non-binding or unenacted in that description. — same release
- Nothing in the retrieved record gives CAISI power to compel pre-release model access, to inspect a developer's systems, to issue or withdraw a licence, or to sanction non-cooperation. Its own funding instrument is a contribution agreement with a third party (CIFAR), not a regulatory power over developers.

**The safety commitment as written.**

- The only safety sentence in the government release is "**Keep working with the Canadian AI Safety Institute** to champion the safe development and use of advanced AI." No deliverable, no schedule, no evidence standard, no recipient for reports, no consequence for failure. The reporting obligation in the same release is expressly scoped to "**jobs and investment in Canada**". — [PM release](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/10/29/canada-announces-landmark-investment-cohere-scale-secure-sovereign-ai)

**The investee's own commercial framing, which is the hook for a commercial-manner-compatible term.**

- Cohere markets itself as "a leading provider of **sovereign AI for regulated industries and governments**" whose platform provides "maximum security, privacy, and deployment flexibility across public and private clouds, on-premises environments, and **fully air-gapped settings**." — [Cohere, PR Newswire, 29 Oct 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/cohere-announces-series-e-fundraise-targeting-cad-7-billion-to-meet-surging-global-demand-for-secure-sovereign-ai-302923951.html)
- The Canadian government's stated rationale is control over data: "Cohere builds secure AI systems for businesses and governments. Its technology gives organisations greater control over where sensitive data is stored, how it is used, and **who can access it**." — [PM release](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/10/29/canada-announces-landmark-investment-cohere-scale-secure-sovereign-ai)

### Inferences

- **Evidenced current duty (what the government can actually enforce today):** progress reports on jobs and domestic compute investment, and a non-binding statement of continued CAISI cooperation. That is the whole of it. There is no verification standard, no reporting recipient named, no cure period, and no remedy — including no stated consequence for missing a stage.
- **Strongest plausible condition that fits the statute:** a **security-and-integrity covenant** in the subscription or shareholders' agreement, with four operative parts: (i) **pre-release evaluation access** — Cohere provides CAISI or an accredited evaluator with pre-deployment access to model versions it supplies to Government of Canada and critical-infrastructure customers, with scope defined by model version and release milestone; (ii) **security-incident notification** to CAISI within a fixed period, with retained evidence and a defined severity scale; (iii) **independent assurance** — an annual third-party report on the security and key-custody controls behind the air-gapped/Model Vault deployment claims; (iv) **remedy** — the covenant is a condition of the next stage release, with a cure period and, failing cure, an information and step-in right.
- **Why this condition and not the others:** it is the only one that can be written as a **customer-assurance warranty about a product Cohere already sells**, which keeps it inside s.10(2)'s "commercial manner" duty, keeps it inside Canada's jurisdiction over a Toronto-incorporated company, and does not require CAISI to acquire powers it does not have. Pre-release testing is defensible on the same footing; incident reporting is even easier. **Protected weights and chip-location inventory** are reachable but belong to the compute commitment, not the equity covenant. **Local stop with due process** is not reachable by equity at all.
- **The honest limit:** a covenant is only as good as the holder's willingness to enforce it. Because s.21 makes the agreement privileged, a coalition could not verify from outside whether the term exists, whether it is being met, or whether a stage was withheld for that reason. **Enforceability and verifiability are separate problems here, and the second one is currently unsolved.**

### Gaps

- **CAISI's 2026 status is not established.** The only primary source read describes November 2024. It is possible CAISI acquired testing functions or agreements since; no evidence of statutory inspection or model-access powers was located, and the "proposed" AIDA status in that release is not proof of its 2026 status either way.
- **No text of any Cohere–CAISI arrangement.** If one exists, it is the single document that would convert "keep working with" into something with defined content.
- **Nothing establishes that any safety term was discussed**, let alone agreed. Every condition above is a proposal, and the "commercial manner" duty argument is a legal reading of the statute, not an opinion from Canadian counsel.

## Q3. Verdict, map-ready edge, and the exact missing evidence

### Takeaway

**Result: PARTIAL.** One link that earlier notes had as unknown — *is there a lawful vehicle with capacity to attach a condition* — is now answered from primary law: yes, CDEV under the CDEV Act. Every downstream link is unestablished: the holder is reported but unnamed in any official document, the tranche structure that determines discretion is unknown, no executed instrument exists in public, CAISI cannot enforce anything, and the statute makes the terms likely permanently non-public. Announced staging is **not** a verified right to renegotiate.

### Cited Findings

- Merger status, as signed but not closed on the primary record: "The transaction **remains subject to final regulatory approvals**" — [Cohere blog, 16 Sep 2026](https://cohere.com/blog/cohere-and-aleph-alpha-sign-agreement); "remains subject to the **required regulatory approvals**" — [Baker McKenzie, Sep 2026](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/newsroom/2026/09/cohere-business-combination-aleph-alpha). Signed 16 September 2026 at a reported ~$20bn valuation, dual headquarters **Toronto and Berlin**, operating globally as Cohere. — [Aleph Alpha, 16 Sep 2026](https://aleph-alpha.com/en/news/cohere-agreement-transatlantic-sovereign-ai/)
- Contradicting secondary claim: "Cohere **in September completed** its acquisition of German AI startup Aleph Alpha GmbH." — [Financial Post, 29 Oct 2026](https://financialpost.com/technology/cohere-raise-billion-ottawa-canadian-ai-company)
- The government release's own phrasing: "Cohere's **recent merger** with Aleph Alpha brings together Canadian and German AI expertise." — [PM release](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/10/29/canada-announces-landmark-investment-cohere-scale-secure-sovereign-ai)
- A secondary outlet headline asserting "C$1.6 Billion Cohere Deal Lets Ottawa **Veto Any Foreign Acquisition — No Law Required**" and "attaching **binding conditions** that give Ottawa ongoing veto power" ([Tech Times, 30 Oct 2026](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329764/20261030/c16-billion-cohere-deal-lets-ottawa-veto-any-foreign-acquisition-no-law-required.htm), headline only). **This is not supported by the primary text**: the government release contains no veto, no reserved matter, no board seat, and no consent right. It states a covenant to "maintain its Canadian incorporation and Canadian headquarters", which is a commitment by the company, not a government approval right over a future acquirer.

### Inferences

- **Merger wording should not be amplified in either direction.** The defensible statement on 31 October 2026 is: *a definitive business combination agreement was signed 16 September 2026, subject to regulatory approval; no closing announcement was located; the government and one newspaper describe it as recent or completed without a primary closing release to cite.* An unlocated closing announcement does not prove the deal failed, and a newspaper's "completed" does not prove it closed.
- **The merger creates a real tension with the announced covenant.** The commitment is to "maintain its Canadian incorporation and Canadian headquarters"; the announced post-merger structure is **dual headquarters in Toronto and Berlin**. Which legal entity is the Canadian one after combination, and whether "Canadian incorporation" can be satisfied by a subsidiary, is unresolved. This is the most concrete way an announced condition could turn out to be hollow, and it is checkable.
- **Verdict reasoning, stated plainly.** *Pass* would need an executed instrument naming a holder and a safety covenant with a remedy. *Fail* would need the discretion to be absent or the statute to forbid the condition. Neither is true: the money is "up to", stages are undisclosed, and the statute permits covenants. So the correct result is **partial**, and the determinant is a single unknown — tranche architecture — that no amount of further desk research will resolve without the agreement or a ministerial statement.

**Why the request is shaped this way.** Item 1 is the document everyone wants and the one the statute is designed to keep private. Item 7 is the under-used route: the government has committed itself to "regular reporting", so a committee can ask for the report without needing the agreement. If the team wants one ask, ask for the reporting and for the tranche architecture, not the contract.
